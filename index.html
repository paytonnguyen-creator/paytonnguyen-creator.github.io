Cognitive science, checked by data.

I'm a double major in Data Science and Cognitive Science at UC Berkeley, with a domain emphasis in Business & Industrial Analytics.

Cognitive science is the study of how people actually decide — the mental models they build, what they attend to, and the systematic ways they get it wrong. Strategy and product work run on exactly those questions: why did this customer leave, what will they do if we change this, which explanation of their behavior is the true one. Most people arrive at those questions through business coursework. I'm arriving through the discipline that studies the behavior itself.

The data science side is the check. It is easy to tell a convincing story about why users do something, and much harder to test whether it survives the evidence. The most useful thing I've learned so far is how often the biggest, most obvious finding turns out to be a confound — and that catching it is the cognitive science instinct, distrust your own inference, and the data science method, statistical control, doing the same job from two directions.

I'm open to strategy, consulting, insights, analytics, operations and the roles next door to them — the common thread is owning a question end to end rather than pulling numbers for someone else's answer. Longer term I'm aiming at product management, but the analyst years are where you find out whether your instincts about people are actually right, and I'd rather earn that transition than skip it.

Outside all of this I play tennis and pickleball, coach juniors, and translate at medical outreach clinics in Vietnam and Little Saigon.

Thirty billing dates

Ten episodes can be released in one night or over ten weeks. The gap between those choices is worth millions, and nobody outside Netflix has the data to settle it — so I built the argument instead of asserting an answer.

  • An interactive simulator, not a write-up — 13,000 simulated households over 182 days, five release patterns run against the same people and the same billing dates, recomputing live in the browser as you drag the assumptions.
  • Found the two objectives rank the patterns in opposite orders: signups follow concentration, renewals follow spread — so the decision is an exchange rate, not a best practice.
  • Found the winning pattern wins by less than the swing from any single assumption, and said so on the page — the tool reports whether the recommendation flips, not just how far the number moves.
  • Tech: JavaScript, agent-based simulation, Web Worker, hand-drawn SVG

Degree Ledger

Berkeley scatters a degree's rules across a major page, a college page, a minor page and a transfer-credit policy, then asks you to reconcile them yourself. This reconciles them in one place — for any L&S major — and tells you what is still standing between you and the walk.

  • Works for any L&S major and any CDSS minor — paste a program's requirements straight off the Academic Guide and the blocks, course lists and cross-listings are read out of the text, or build the blocks by hand.
  • One click for transfer students: say your IGETC or Cal-GETC certification is filed and eleven requirement blocks close themselves, with the four things certification doesn't cover listed rather than left to be discovered later.
  • Enforces the rules that actually catch people: unit ceilings, upper-division minimums, the courses a major and a minor may not both count, and the extra bar a second degree adds.
  • Runs entirely in the browser and saves to the device — no account, no server, nothing about anyone's transcript leaving their laptop.
  • Tech: React, esbuild, client-side state, no backend

Why 62% of customers never came back

Should the next unit of retention effort go into a model that predicts who returns, or a treatment applied to everyone?

  • Analysed the real CDNOW transaction log — 23,570 customers, 67,591 orders — chosen for a cohort design where the repeat window is fully observed for every customer.
  • Found the first order barely predicts return (hold-out AUC 0.576), and that the largest apparent lever collapsed by 82% under statistical control — so it was rejected rather than recommended.
  • Recommended a timed second-purchase program over a propensity model, with the opportunity sized and explicit kill criteria in a PRD.
  • Tech: Python, pandas, scikit-learn

The 92% that wasn't

A vendor demos a support AI at 92.4% accuracy. What share of a live queue can actually be handled with no human — and is the next dollar better spent on the model or on the routing around it?

  • Rebuilt the business case on a benchmark that includes 1,200 requests the system was never designed to handle — the traffic most intent datasets leave out, and the reason demo numbers don't survive a real queue.
  • Found the abstention rule worth $211k per 100,000 contacts against $50k for a flawless classifier, so the recommendation was to spend first on handoff design rather than model quality.
  • Showed the business-case number exceeds the physical ceiling by 23%, and that the obvious health metric improves as the queue degrades — a monitoring trap, with the fix in the risk register.
  • Tech: Python, scikit-learn, pandas

The pickleball bottleneck

Open play rations a scarce court by making people queue. What does that queue actually cost, and can telling players in advance move any of it?

  • Simulated 843,581 court visits across five parks over four years of real NOAA weather, keeping the assumed layer and the emergent layer strictly separate — and labelling on every chart which one it is.
  • Found the queue hides its own congestion: 6× the demand gives 9× the wait but 52× the walk-aways, so counting the line measures only the survivors — and that darkness, not crowding, causes the most wasted trips.
  • Established an irreducible forecast error of 2.18 minutes, so the product ships four states rather than a number — then recommended a free rotation-rule change that beats my own app and buys 75% of a court expansion.
  • Tech: Python, pandas, scikit-learn, discrete-event simulation

How I work through a problem.

All three case studies above follow four beats, and so does everything else I work on — a habit borrowed from research method: say what would prove you wrong before you look. Analysis isn't finished until it changes what someone does.

  1. 01

    Frame the real question

    The stated problem is rarely the actual problem. Before touching data I write down what decision this is meant to inform, and what answer would change it.

  2. 02

    Interrogate the data

    Clean it, then try to break my own hypothesis. Confirmation bias shows up fastest in your own charts.

  3. 03

    Find the mechanism

    A correlation isn't an insight. I push until I can name a plausible reason people behave the way the numbers say, and state what would disprove it.

  4. 04

    Make the call

    One recommendation, the tradeoff it carries, and how you'd know within a quarter whether it worked.
