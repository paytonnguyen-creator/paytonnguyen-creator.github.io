Thirty billing dates — what a release pattern is actually worth

Outer Banks ends with season five. Almost every decision about it is already sunk — the cast, the scripts, the budget. The release pattern is one of the last levers that costs nothing to pull and still moves the P&L. So I built the thing that would let a PM argue about it: a simulator you can disagree with, in public, by dragging its assumptions.

Role
Solo — framing, model, simulator, recommendation
Built with
JavaScript, agent-based simulation, hand-drawn SVG
Scale
13,000 simulated households × 182 days × 5 patterns, live in the browser
Type
Decision tool, not a forecast

The decision

A streaming service can release ten episodes in one night, in two halves, or one a week for ten weeks. It is a genuinely contested question inside the industry, and the interesting part is that both camps are arguing about different metrics without saying so.

The public record for this show is short and useful. Seasons one through four each ran ten episodes. Season four was split, with the first five landing on 3 October 2024 and the second five on 7 November — thirty-five days later. Netflix has used the same two-part shape for several tentpoles, including Stranger Things 4 and Bridgerton 3. And season five has been announced as the last one, which quietly removes the usual justification for stretching a release out: there is no next season to hold anyone for.

What is real here, and what is not

Netflix publishes a weekly Top 10 and a twice-yearly engagement report. It does not publish churn, it does not publish the distribution of billing dates, it does not publish per-title cohort behaviour, and since 2025 it no longer reports quarterly subscriber counts at all. There is no dataset behind this project, and pretending otherwise would be the whole failure mode.

The rule I built the project around

Every behavioural number in the model is an assumption, and every assumption a reader could reasonably dispute is exposed as a control rather than buried in the code. What the model contributes is mechanism, not measurement: if you accept how the pieces fit together, the tool tells you what follows. If you don't, it lets you change the pieces and watch the recommendation move — which it does, for three of them.

The mechanism

Subscribers do not cancel continuously. They cancel on their renewal date, which happens once a month, and those dates are spread roughly evenly across the calendar. So on any given day, about one subscriber in thirty is standing at the only decision point that matters. Everyone else is unreachable — you can delight them, but you cannot bill them differently for it.

That reframes the question. A release pattern does not change how many people want to watch a season. It changes when their viewing hours land, and therefore how much "I've been watching this thing" each subscriber is carrying on the day their card gets charged. Ten episodes dropped at once produce an enormous amount of that feeling, concentrated in about four days, and then it decays. Ten episodes dropped weekly produce much less of it at any moment, but they keep producing it for nine weeks.

Which of those is worth more is not obvious, it depends on numbers nobody outside the company has, and it is exactly the sort of argument that gets settled by whoever is most confident in the room. So: build the model, expose the assumptions, and let the argument be about the assumptions.

Attack the assumptions

None of these are measured. They are the numbers the whole answer rests on, so they belong in the interface rather than in the source. Two of them — the halo and the concentration premium — are load-bearing enough that moving either one changes which pattern wins.

What would change the recommendation

A tornado chart of the wrong kind tells you how much the number moves. This one also tells you whether the call moves — whether a different pattern would win if the assumption were wrong in either direction. That is the only sensitivity question a decision-maker actually has.

About 200 more release windows. Takes a couple of seconds.

Is the winning margin even real?

The leading patterns finish close together, and a simulation has its own sampling noise. This re-runs all five against eight independently drawn audiences and reports the paired difference between the top two — same people, same coin flips, different policy — because comparing two averages from correlated draws would flatter whichever one won.

Eight full re-runs. Takes a few seconds.

Three things the model says

The two objectives rank the patterns in opposite orders

At the default settings, the pattern that maximises acquisition is all at once, and the pattern that maximises retention is premiere plus weekly. They sit at opposite ends of the same list. That is not a coincidence or a modelling accident — it falls out of two mechanisms pointing in opposite directions.

Signups follow concentration. A crowded opening week is what buys the Top 10 slot, the press cycle and the social feed, and all three reward simultaneity rather than cumulative hours. Retention follows spread, because renewal dates are spread, and a single spike of engagement can only sit near some of them.

Which means the honest framing of the decision is not "binge or weekly." It is: how much acquisition are you willing to sell for how much retention — and the answer depends on whether the business is currently short of subscribers or short of loyalty. That is a strategy question, and the model can only tell you the exchange rate.

The winner wins by an amount too small to defend

A two-part split comes out ahead, which is also what the show actually did last season. But the margin over second place is around $1M against a $28M marketing line — smaller than the swing from any single assumption in the sensitivity panel, and only a little larger than the model's own variation between audience draws. Run the stability check and it wins seven times out of eight, by a paired mean under a million dollars.

So the tool's most useful output is not the ranking. It is the finding that the top three patterns are, on money, a tie — which means anyone presenting one of them as the clear financial winner is reading precision that isn't there. Once the money is a tie, the decision should be made on something else, and the decision rule I fixed before running anything says what: take the option that produces the most evidence for next time.

The savable audience is much smaller than the reach

The season reaches around 39% of the modelled subscriber base, but on its best day it reduces cancellations by under 10%. The gap is not wastage — it is overlap. The people most likely to watch a returning Netflix tentpole are heavy users, and heavy users were not going to cancel this month anyway. The people on the edge of leaving are light users, and light users are the hardest to get into episode one.

This is the single most transferable thing in the project, and it has nothing to do with television: a retention programme aimed at your most engaged segment is mostly paying to keep people who were staying. Any business case that multiplies "reach" by "churn reduction among people we reached" is double-counting unless it checks that overlap first.

Where the model disagrees with the real decision

Sweep the gap between parts and it peaks at about three weeks. The actual gap last season was five. The model prefers three because a shorter gap keeps the buzz channel compounding while still landing the second part after the first one's engagement has decayed enough to be worth refreshing; by five weeks it is paying for the decay without buying much extra coverage.

I think the model is wrong and the real calendar is right, and it is worth being specific about why rather than quietly adjusting a parameter until my answer matches. Three things it does not know: a marketing organisation cannot mount two genuine campaigns three weeks apart; the competitive calendar has holes in it that a release has to fit; and post-production on the back half of a season delivers when it delivers. The gap curve is nearly flat between two and five weeks anyway — a $1.6M spread — so the operational constraints should win. A model that overrides a scheduling reality it never encoded is not being rigorous, it is being naive.

What would change the call

Three assumptions flip the recommendation outright, and one of them carries the entire argument for spreading a release out. I call it the halo: the extra viewing of everything else that someone does while they are mid-season on a show they are following. Set it to zero in the drawer and releasing all at once wins. Set it high and weekly climbs from last place to competitive.

Nobody outside Netflix can observe that number, and I could not find a defensible public estimate, so I chose one. Every conclusion here that favours spreading a release out is downstream of a number I made up — which is precisely why it is a slider on the page instead of a constant in the source, and why the tool reports whether the call flips rather than only how far the total moves.

How you'd know it worked

Primary metric: subscriber-months retained per thousand viewers reached, measured against the same window in the prior season, split by whether the viewer's renewal date fell inside or outside the release window. That split is the whole design — the outside-window group is the control, and it is free. Target: the inside-window group retains at least 4 percentage points better. Kill criterion: under 1.5 points, the coverage mechanism this entire model rests on does not exist, and release patterning should be handed back to marketing as a pure awareness decision. Counter-metric: thirty-day retention of the acquisition cohort, because a pattern that maximises signups by concentrating them also delivers them all to the same first renewal with nothing left to watch.

What I'd do differently

  • The concentration premium is doing too much work and I can't verify it. One exponent decides that a crowd arriving at once is worth more than the same crowd spread thin, and it sets which pattern wins acquisition. It is defensible — every ranking and press cycle rewards simultaneity — but it is a judgement, and the model would be better if it were fitted against public Top 10 data for split releases rather than asserted.
  • I gave split releases a second marketing campaign and then found that split releases do well. The budget weighting is a real feature of how launches work, but it is an input, not a finding, and it flatters the pattern that wins. Turn the awareness-per-dollar assumption up and this shows its hand — the ranking starts moving for a reason that has nothing to do with billing dates.
  • Uniform renewal dates are too convenient. Real billing dates cluster, because signups clustered when past hits landed. A base whose renewal dates are already piled up near previous premiere anniversaries would make coverage a much richer problem than the flat one I modelled, and might well change the optimal gap.
  • This model reproduced a decision I already knew the answer to. Two parts, which is what the show actually did. That is the kind of agreement that should make you suspicious rather than pleased — I built the thing knowing the answer, and there is no version of this where I can fully rule out having steered toward it. The defence is that the mechanisms were fixed before the numbers were tuned and the disagreement about the gap length was left in rather than fixed. It is a defence, not a proof.
  • Nothing here models the finale. A final season's real strategic question is not retention during the run — it is where the audience goes afterwards, and I reduced that to a single checkbox. The handoff is the most interesting decision on this show and it got the least modelling.
